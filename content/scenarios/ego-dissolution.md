## How to read this page

I'm an author and educator, not a clinician or researcher. This page is adapted from Scenario 12 of my book and revised in September 2026 to separate three kinds of statement: what published research shows, marked **Research** and numbered to the sources at the end; what people commonly report, marked **Phenomenology**; and what I take from both, marked **My interpretation**. It is educational, not medical advice, and it is not a guide to obtaining or using psilocybin.

## What ego dissolution is

**Research.** Ego dissolution is a recognised, measurable feature of the psychedelic state. The Ego-Dissolution Inventory, an eight-item questionnaire, was validated on more than 1,800 drug experiences reported by nearly 700 people; the items formed a single coherent factor that was specific to psychedelics rather than to intoxication in general, and its intensity scaled with the intensity of the experience [1]. A philosophical and neuroscientific review in 2017 gathered the phenomenology across classic psychedelics, dissociatives and some other drugs, and treated "drug-induced ego dissolution" as a distinct phenomenon worth studying in its own right [2].

**My interpretation.** In plain language: ego dissolution is not losing your memory or your identity in the medical sense. It is the temporary quieting of the process that normally builds the experience of being a separate "me" who is reading these words.

## Why it happens, as far as research can say

**Research.** The most-cited explanation involves the default mode network, a set of brain regions active in self-referential thinking and autobiographical memory. In 2012, Carhart-Harris and colleagues found that psilocybin reduced activity in, and connectivity within, that network, and that the reduction correlated with the intensity of the subjective effects [3].

The picture has grown more complicated since. A 2015 study tied psilocybin-induced ego dissolution to reduced connectivity between the medial temporal lobe and high-level cortical regions, to disintegration of the salience network, and to reduced communication between the hemispheres [4]. A 2016 study of LSD found that ego dissolution correlated with an *increase* in global connectivity across the brain, in regions rich in the serotonin 2A receptor that psychedelics act on [5]. A 2019 theoretical model, REBUS, proposes that psychedelics relax the precision of the brain's high-level beliefs, including the belief in a bounded self [6]; it is a framework, not a finding.

**My interpretation.** The brain's model of a separate self appears to depend on coordination across several networks. Psychedelics disturb that coordination in more than one way. "The default mode network switches off" is a memorable summary, and it is not wrong, but it is not the whole mechanism, and researchers who study this do not agree on how central it is.

## What it can feel like

**Phenomenology.** Reports vary, and one person's dissolution can be gentle while another's is total. Commonly described:

- **The inner narrator goes quiet.** The voice that comments on everything stops, or becomes distant.
- **Boundaries soften.** The line between your body and the room, between you and another person, between you and the music, becomes porous or vanishes.
- **Time stops behaving.** Minutes can feel like hours, or hours can collapse into a moment.
- **Identity becomes optional.** Your name, your history, your roles feel like one possible costume among many rather than the only truth.
- **A sense of unity.** Many people describe a felt sense that all things are one, or that there is only awareness, or that something they would call sacred is present and close.
- **Sometimes fear.** Especially the first time. The mind that constructs the self often interprets its own dissolution as dying. The fear is real. By itself, the dissolution is not known to be physically harmful, and for most people it passes as the effects fade.

## How long it lasts

**Research.** No study has timed ego dissolution as a separate phase, so any precise figure would be invented. What is known is the overall time course. In controlled studies of oral psilocybin at roughly 15 to 30 mg, subjective effects begin within the first hour, peak around one and a half to two and a half hours after taking it, and have largely faded by about six hours; higher doses produce stronger and somewhat longer effects [7][8][9]. Ego dissolution is usually reported around the peak.

**My interpretation.** Expect the dissolution to sit somewhere inside that window, to arrive as the effects intensify, and to ease as they fade. How long it feels is a different matter. Time distortion is part of the experience, and an hour can feel like far longer.

## Is it harmful?

**Research.** Classic psychedelics are considered relatively safe physiologically at the doses used in research, and are not associated with dependence, but the same safety review that says so is explicit that they carry unique psychological risks, and it sets out screening and support practices to reduce them: excluding people with a personal or family history of psychotic disorders or bipolar disorder, preparing participants in advance, and having trained companions present throughout [10].

Outside research settings, a survey of nearly 2,000 people describing their single most difficult psilocybin experience found that 24 percent reported anxiety, paranoia, depression or fear lasting a week or longer afterward, 10 percent reported symptoms lasting more than a year, and 7.6 percent of those whose experience was more than a year old had sought treatment for enduring symptoms; three cases were associated with the onset of enduring psychotic symptoms. In the same survey, 84 percent said they had benefited from the experience [11]. Because respondents were asked about their worst experience, these figures describe what can happen, not how often it happens overall.

A 2020 systematic review of 34 studies with follow-ups of at least two weeks found that lasting changes after psychedelics were mostly in the direction of improved mood, wellbeing and personality measures, in populations that had been screened and supported [12].

**My interpretation.** Ego dissolution itself is not the danger. Unpreparedness, an unsafe setting, being alone, and pre-existing vulnerability are where the risk concentrates. I write for people making sense of an experience they have already had; if you are considering one, the screening practices in the safety literature are the standard to hold yourself to, and this page is not a substitute for them.

## When to seek qualified help

**My interpretation, informed by the safety literature.** Get help from a qualified professional, sooner rather than later, if:

- A feeling of being **detached from yourself or from reality** continues in the days after the experience rather than fading, or is getting stronger. Persisting depersonalization or derealization after psychedelics is reported but not well studied, and it is a reason to be seen, not to wait.
- The experience surfaced material connected to **trauma**, and it is not settling, or you find yourself avoiding it in ways that are costing you.
- You experience **anything resembling psychosis**: fixed beliefs others don't share, hearing or seeing things others don't, or severe confusion. Seek help promptly. Do not wait to see if it passes.
- You feel **persistently disconnected** from yourself, your relationships or basic functioning.

An integration-informed therapist is the right first call for the first two. Psychotic symptoms need urgent medical care. This page is not medical advice and does not replace either.

## What I take from this

**My interpretation.** Three things guide the navigation steps above. First, non-resistance: if the sense of self is going to soften regardless, fighting it converts a strange experience into a frightening one. Second, an anchor in sensation: the body is still there when the narrator isn't, and breath or the floor is a bridge back that doesn't require a "me" to use it. Third, a small witness: naming what is fading keeps a thread of awareness that can watch without needing to act. None of this comes from a trial. It comes from working with my own experiences and with the reports of others, and it is offered as a starting point, not a rule.

## Integration afterward

**My interpretation.** The experience is not the work. The work is what you do with it over the weeks and months that follow. An ego-dissolution experience that is not integrated tends to fade into a beautiful memory; one that is integrated tends to slowly change how you live. Write soon, return to what you wrote, spend time in your body, and talk with someone who has held these conversations before. Give large decisions weeks, not days.
